A. Introduction

I have posted on a few places about my attempts and journey across Regulatory Body examinations. Facing a variety of questions, I have decided to make this document that all can freely check out. For any questions, remarks, or just for a chat, please feel free to message me on Reddit (u/KSameer73) or Telegram (@dxrsam_0).

Nonetheless, I don’t have much to say. This is not much of a guide, but a catalogue of my experiences that some may enjoy, relate with, or benefit from. I won’t give certain details, such as all of my exact marks, as I’ve been advised against that.

I have been selected in the RBI Grade B (DSIM) recruitment in the 2025 cycle, at the age of 28. My background is:

  • Integrated M.Sc. in Mathematics from a mid-tier institute
  • MBA from a Tier 1 institute
  • 1 year of work experience [not elaborating]

B. RBI Grade B (Generalist) preparation, 2025

B.1. Phase I

The notification came out on 10 Sep 2025, and Phase I was on 18 Oct.

I did not prepare for Phase I. However, since I had diligently studied for that the previous year (Panel Year 2024), I was familiar with most of the static portion. My efforts were rather concentrated entirely on the DSIM (q.v.) Phase I, which was on the very next day (19 Oct)!

It turned out that the pattern of the exam’s GA section had been overturned this year. There was a heavy reduction in RBI-related technical areas and an increase in “general current affairs” – which included things like geography and history. This was akin to striking gold for me, as I had some familiarity with these subjects; moreover, it would have put focused aspirants at a relative disadvantage, leveling the playing field.

I did not prepare at all for the QRE (Quants, Reasoning, and English) sections either. Earlier CAT prep (preceding my MBA) as well as a strong English were enough to see me through. Attaching the marks.

It should be obvious that you should NOT take my experience too seriously, as a lot of factors (not in the least, luck) happened to align for me. In particular, note that the sectional cutoffs were 30% (UR), so I barely scraped by in Quants, and cleared the aggregate cutoff (77.5) by just 2 marks.

B.2. Phase II

I got selected in Phase I in both the Generalist stream and DSIM. In my head I had performed rather poorly in DSIM, so I decided to prepare for the Generalist stream only, in Phase II. Here, again, the two exams were to be on adjacent dates – Generalist on 6 Dec 2025 and DSIM on 7 Dec 2025.

How did I prepare? I needed to cover a lot of GA as I had studied nothing for Phase I. But I wasn’t massively bothered about the static portion as I felt familiar (though not confident) with almost all of it – Finance, Management, and Economics had made up a large part of my MBA curriculum. I went through  all topics given in the syllabus and all PYQs, which gave me an idea of what all is important and what’s not. Then I used Gemini to learn everything I felt lacking in. It worked best to give as detailed a prompt as possible, and telling it to focus only on one little point in one response.

As for the dynamic portion, I studied from monthly compilations of news and government schemes. I won’t take the name of the coaching institute whose material I used, but I have had the chance to look at several, and they all seemed fine to me. I used to make crisp notes in Obsidian, making it easy to revise. It syncs on PC and phone (I had an Android), so that I could make the most of my time anywhere.

IMO the most important aspect of Phase II is writing skills, i.e. to structure your answer appropriately and cogently in the limited time provided. Here my reading and writing habits came in handy – as I have been writing both short-form and long-form content semi-regularly on Twitter and other platforms for as long as I can recall. Reading the newspaper (especially the editorial pages) semi-regularly also came in handy, as you develop a sense of what a “good essay” is like, and get familiar with various styles of exposition. This also works as a guide for the interview. It’s difficult to advise on how an aspirant should study in the short term, but continual writing practice is certainly crucial.

I will report the discussion, as far as I can recall, in chronological order and in a polished way. Post hoc comments will be in blue.

There were 5 interviewers in the panel. I’ll be numbering the interviewer (from the left) wherever I remember the right person (mostly not). The guy in the middle seemed to be the seniormost. Not all were RBI folks: it's common to have people from the government and public sector banks in the panel.

I scored 50 out of 75 in the interview. Among the candidates selected for the post, this is an average score. I missed the final cutoff by 3.25 marks — meaning I under-scored in Phase II.

C. RBI Grade B (DSIM) preparation, 2025

C.1. Phase I + Phase II

The recruitments for DSIM (Department of Statistics and Information Management) and DEPR are conducted concurrently with the Generalist recruitment by the RBI. The eligibility criterion for DSIM is an M.Sc. in Statistics or Mathematics, among others [please check the notification if interested]. The exam syllabus mostly follows B.Sc. (Statistics), with topics from AI/ML and Database Management added since Panel Year 2025.

My background is in Mathematics, not Statistics.

Realizing that the exam syllabus is actually just of the B.Sc. level and NOT the M.Sc. level (which one might suspect from the eligibility criteria), it was not overly difficult to cover for me. I should highlight the following aspects:

  1. The Statistics folks anyway spend a lot of time learning just Math – be it real analysis (≈ calculus), measure theory, or linear algebra. I was already comfortable with these topics.
  2. Measure theory, in particular, is important in college-level Math to build the theory of the (Lebesgue) integral. Since probability spaces are simply a special type of measure space, many probability-theoretic concepts were readily accessible to me.
  3. Probability theory is also central in quantum mechanics, on which I had several courses in my Integrated M.Sc., and in which I have always maintained a personal interest. Projection-valued measures are also developed here.
  4. I had studied Probability & Statistics as one course in my Integrated M.Sc., and further in my M.B.A.

This means the functional skills to handle and manipulate probability distributions and statistical tests were already there with me, albeit in a quirky mixture of quintessential rigor (coming from pure math) and superficial applications (coming from M.B.A.). The mid-part – consisting of the development of statistical frameworks and tools – was missing, and will remain incomplete for years if not decades.

Anyhow, the method of preparation was twofold:

  1. Find out as many PYQs as possible and understand their solutions fully, so as to be able to tackle questions on those patterns. I extensively used Gemini for this, and it was accurate and helpful 90% of the time. My biggest challenge with Gemini was that it tended to miss the easy path through a problem, and to neglect telling me important things that were not explicitly asked for.
  2. Study some topics from the books. I couldn’t find one suitable book on Statistics that I enjoyed – but I used these somewhat: [DETAILS TO BE FILLED].

The PYQs used were of the ISS (Indian Statistical Service), CSIR-NET, and GATE.

I feel my 2024 preparation for RBI Grade B (Generalist) as well as NABARD Grade A and IRDAI Grade A somewhat helped me in 2025 as well. In what way? Mostly in terms of knowing what all to study and what all to skip.

E. Other questions asked to me on Reddit etc.

Q 1. Did work experience help in the selection?

Honestly, I doubt that any marks would be assigned to work experience of the candidates. But in terms of confidence and ability to communicate in the interview, it helped. Hard to quantify.

If you have a mature conversational style out of college itself, there's nothing to be concerned about. If not, some practice with friends will be desirable.

Q 2. What’s the difference between the Generalist and DSIM work profiles? Which exam did you find to be easier?

Loosely speaking, in the Generalist stream they can put you into any department, but Supervision is the most common one AFAIK.

In DSIM, you always stay in DSIM (Department of Statistics and Information Management). Limited scope of work and requires specialized knowledge. Preference heavily dependent on interest.

I found the difficulties of both to be in the same ballpark, as I made roughly the same amount of silly mistakes in both, and fell short of some low-hanging fruit to roughly the same extent in both. Like I could easily have gotten 10 more marks in ESI, I could have gotten 10 more marks in DSIM Phase I [and remember, DSIM adds Phase I marks in calculating the final marks]. And I nearly made it in the Generalist stream as well, missing the final cutoff by merely 3 marks.

Q 2. Do gap years matter? Does X Y Z degree or certification matter?

No and no.

But be ready to defend your decisions and your aptitude on anything you let slip. The interviewers will drill down on anything that catches their eyes.

From your graduation (and post-graduation, if any) degrees, please have a schematic understanding of the field as a whole, and in addition an in-depth understanding of how it touches on matters of interest to the RBI – such as economics, statistics, and geopolitics – and possibly on day-to-day matters. In my case (see Section B.3), they asked me to tell them about some interesting idea in maths that they could understand: I had to take a moment but came up with something decent, but there were better answers possible; I could have brought up game theory (which underlies decision making in all sorts of scenarios) or optimization theory (which plays a role in economics, to find optimal values of, say, the repo rate). Once the discussion entered such a topic, I could have spoken vigorously on that.

A. Introduction

I have posted on a few places about my attempts and journey across Regulatory Body examinations. Facing a variety of questions, I have decided to make this document that all can freely check out. For any questions, remarks, or just for a chat, please feel free to message me on Reddit (u/KSameer73) or Telegram (@dxrsam_0).

Nonetheless, I don’t have much to say. This is not much of a guide, but a catalogue of my experiences that some may enjoy, relate with, or benefit from. I won’t give certain details, such as all of my exact marks, as I’ve been advised against that.

I have been selected in the RBI Grade B (DSIM) recruitment in the 2025 cycle, at the age of 28. My background is:

  • Integrated M.Sc. in Mathematics from a mid-tier institute
  • MBA from a Tier 1 institute
  • 1 year of work experience [not elaborating]

My journey across the Regulatory Body examinations is summarized in the following table.

B. RBI Grade B (Generalist) preparation, 2025

B.1. Phase I

The notification came out on 10 Sep 2025, and Phase I was on 18 Oct.

I did not prepare for Phase I. However, since I had diligently studied for that the previous year (Panel Year 2024), I was familiar with most of the static portion. My efforts were rather concentrated entirely on the DSIM (q.v.) Phase I, which was on the very next day (19 Oct)!

It turned out that the pattern of the exam’s GA section had been overturned this year. There was a heavy reduction in RBI-related technical areas and an increase in “general current affairs” – which included things like geography and history. This was akin to striking gold for me, as I had some familiarity with these subjects; moreover, it would have put focused aspirants at a relative disadvantage, leveling the playing field.

I did not prepare at all for the QRE (Quants, Reasoning, and English) sections either. Earlier CAT prep (preceding my MBA) as well as a strong English were enough to see me through. Arun Sharma's books had sufficed, but to get high marks proper mock tests will be needed. Attaching the marks.

It should be obvious that you should NOT take my experience too seriously, as a lot of factors (not in the least, luck) happened to align for me. In particular, note that the sectional cutoffs were 30% (UR), so I barely scraped by in Quants, and cleared the aggregate cutoff (77.5) by just 2 marks.

B.2. Phase II

I got selected in Phase I in both the Generalist stream and DSIM. In my head I had performed rather poorly in DSIM, so I decided to prepare for the Generalist stream only, in Phase II. Here, again, the two exams were to be on adjacent dates – Generalist on 6 Dec 2025 and DSIM on 7 Dec 2025.

How did I prepare? I needed to cover a lot of GA as I had studied nothing for Phase I. But I wasn’t massively bothered about the static portion as I felt familiar (though not confident) with almost all of it – Finance, Management, and Economics had made up a large part of my MBA curriculum. I went through  all topics given in the syllabus and all PYQs, which gave me an idea of what all is important and what’s not. Then I used Gemini to learn everything I felt lacking in. It worked best to give as detailed a prompt as possible, and telling it to focus only on one little point in one response.

As for the dynamic portion, I studied from monthly compilations of news and government schemes. I won’t take the name of the coaching institute whose material I used, but I have had the chance to look at several, and they all seemed fine to me. I used to make crisp notes in Obsidian, making it easy to revise. It syncs on PC and phone (I had an Android), so that I could make the most of my time anywhere.

IMO the most important aspect of Phase II is writing skills, i.e. to structure your answer appropriately and cogently in the limited time provided. Here my reading and writing habits came in handy – as I have been writing both short-form and long-form content semi-regularly on Twitter and other platforms for as long as I can recall. Reading the newspaper (especially the editorial pages) semi-regularly also came in handy, as you develop a sense of what a “good essay” is like, and get familiar with various styles of exposition. This also works as a guide for the interview. It’s difficult to advise on how an aspirant should study in the short term, but continual writing practice is certainly crucial.

How should we come up with an answer? There are many frameworks one can follow, but a basic one that always works is the PESTEL framework. This will surely help you come up with a few points to discuss in the answer in detail. The answer should finally be closed out with a nice summarizing sentence alongwith.

B.3. Interview

I will report the discussion, as far as I can recall, in chronological order and in a polished way. Post hoc comments will be in blue.

There were 5 interviewers in the panel. I’ll be numbering the interviewer (from the left) wherever I remember the right person (mostly not). Please don't make too much of the lengths or exact phrasing of my answers here, as I'm writing from memory several months after the interview (though it's something I’ve mulled over and discussed with friends numerous times). The interview lasted 25-30 minutes.

The guy in the middle seemed to be the seniormost. Not all were RBI folks: it's common to have people from the government and public sector banks in the panel.

I scored 50 out of 75 in the interview. Among the candidates selected for the post, this is an average score. I missed the final cutoff by 3 marks — meaning I had somewhat under-scored in Phase II.

1. A quick introduction

As I entered the interview room, my heart rate sped up to 150 (rough guess), but after a minute or two it thankfully slowed down to normal.

I made use of hand gestures throughout the interview – at least once in each response. Not indicating this everywhere below. I was also making sure to look toward everyone in the course of a response, and not just at the person that asked me the question. I would vary the pace, and even nod or smile here and there, to elicit some gesture from them as well. This made the whole process very convivial, in my opinion,

Interviewer: So, Sameer, tell us about yourself.

Me: I am Sameer Kumar, hailing from Hazaribagh in Jharkhand. I've done Integrated M.Sc. from Visva Bharati University in West Bengal — which was founded by Rabindranath Tagore — and MBA from IIM Calcutta last year. Since then I’ve been working in an IT company in a Learning & Development role.

2. About my work-ex

Interviewer: Where are you working now? What does the company do?

Me: I’m working at [company], which is an IT company in [city]. Most of its revenue comes through digital transformation.

Interviewer #2: Digital transformation? What's that?

Me: All the firms have a large amount of data that they need to store and constantly access. Traditionally this was done in large computers managed by the firm itself. Nowadays the trend is to put it all on the cloud, as that adds flexibility and reduces headache on the firm’s part.

My company [company] helps others do that, as it has tie-ups with Google, AWS, and others who actually own the cloud servers and computational resources.

Interviewer: And what's your function there?

Me: I'm in Learning & Development, so I look into certifications like AWS’s Cloud Architect that our engineers are supposed to get, as it improves the impression of the company in front of clients. I also assign and track learning material to the engineers.

Interviewer: That's quite a booming business in the modern world, isn't it? Where do you want to move out of that?

Another interviewer: You're from IIM Calcutta; that's a very premier institute. The placements must be great. How much are you making?

Me: Sir, it's [amount] per annum.

[*continues*] In a few ways it's not a very satisfying job for me. Firstly, it's kind of routine and system-heavy in the way learning tasks are structured, with not much room for creativity given that I’m the only person in the “team” for quite a while. Secondly, the impact is at most company-wide, and that too is hard to pinpoint, because smart people tend to be looking to improve their skills anyway.

Interviewer #2: Impact, you say!

I kinda felt like I pulled myself down into a ditch, at this point. But it was manageable, phew! Cringe diplomatic answer upcoming.

Me: It’s true that a private company, especially in technology, can make large strides and change our lives, but my role is connected rather indirectly to its success. But at the RBI, I feel I may be closer to (and have some say in) the levers that have such a magnified effect on the lives of billions. The mechanism of doing that by tweaking the repo rate, for example, is quite fascinating to me.

3. About economics

Interviewer: In your MBA at IIM Calcutta, what was your specialization?

Me: None, sir. It's a general management degree, but I did have to pick electives in the second year. I took electives from quite a few domains, Most of my electives were from economics and organizational behavior.

Mentioning this was my biggest mistake, as you'll see. I felt pressured to pick out some domain, as that would allow them to ask further from that. I should have avoided mentioning economics, as my economics knowledge was fragmented (and nothing compared to B.Sc. Economics or something). MBA really isn't a very deep course, even though I had 3 electives from Economics (in addition to 3 papers in the 1st year).

Interviewer #3: Economics!! [At this point, AT LEAST three of them exclaimed.] That's great, then you must know about the Solow model?

Me: I’m sorry, I cannot recall this model.

Two of the interviewers: You can’t recall the Solow model?

Me: I’m sure I’ve heard his name, but I cannot remember any details of his theory.

Interviewer #4: You’ve heard of Solow? Can you spell the name?

Me: Sir, S O L O W.

In hindsight, we know this was a good question, because it’s pronounced just like “solo”. This proved I was not lying, and must have saved me from losing even more marks.

Interviewer: Right! So what’s the model about? Capital, population, do these ring a bell?

Me: Sir, I’m really sorry, but even if I studied it, I’ve lost all memory of it.

Interviewer 1: Okay, okay, then tell us about something in economics that you liked.

Me: Please allow me to think for a moment.

I was quite rattled by this event, so I needed some time to calm myself down. Nonetheless, I wasn’t able to come up with something I was comfortable with yet non-trivial, so I went with the following.

Me: Sir, in economics a lot of facts can be derived from axioms or principles. What I find fascinating is items that instead emerge from the data – like the fact that the unemployment rate is negatively correlated with inflation.

Interviewer 3: You’re talking about the Phillips curve. What inference do you draw from it in the Indian context?

Me: Sir, the Indian economy does obey the relationship, but it has not been followed strongly. The connection between these two variables is quite complex and wavering.

My answer was basically right but too superficial.

4. About Anthropic and AI

Interviewer #5: Have you heard of Claude? It has been in the news recently.

(This interview was happening in March 2025.)

Me: *nods*

Interviewer #5: Tell me about it.

Me: So Anthropic [*gestures with the left hand*] has come up with agentic AI models that are able to execute many of the tasks for which companies across the globe have relied on IT firms, especially Indian IT firms. This has created a risky situation for the future of these firms, and large layoffs have started.

Interviewer #2: So what will be their future?

Me: These companies can’t go on doing the same things they have been doing. They will need to adapt to the new environment and make use of these new tools to modernize. But layoffs will continue for now; I can’t see a way around that in the near future.

Interviewer #2: How do you see your company, in this context?

Me: It faces the same risk, ma’am. We’re not sure if we’ll continue to have the some projects that we are on now.

Interviewer #2: Well, if AI is here, you can keep upgrading your knowledge and skills to  remain on the top of things!

Me: Yes, in fact I enjoy doing that. But I can’t say if my company will survive.

This was unnecessarily tongue-in-cheek of me.

5. About the trade war and green energy

Interviewer #1: So tell me about the most important recent geopolitical event.

At some point prior to this I had mentioned an interest in geopolitics, which triggered the initial question.

Me: Sir, are you talking about COVID?

Interviewer #1: No, not that long ago.

Me: Sir, are you referring to the Russia-Ukraine War?

Interviewer #1: No, no, something much more recent.

Me: Oh, you must be referring to Trump’s policies!

Interviewer #1: Yes, exactly! So what has he been doing?

Me: Well, tariffs have been raised on numerous products from India and other countries.

Interviewer #1: Is that bad? What can we do about it?

Me: It’s bad monetarily when seen for what it is. But it also allows us a certain space to develop more capabilities for ourselves and become resilient.

Interviewer #3: The Prime Minister recently talked about Viksit Bharat 2047. In that there was a large focus on green energy; what’s your take on that?

Me: Sir, we are surely on our way to a brighter future, but green energy is not as simple as it seems. For example, when it comes to solar energy, India is able to engage in only the last stage of manufacturing solar panels – which is module assembly and framing. All the previous stages, including polysilicon production and wafer slicing, are done by China and we merely import the wafers from them. So, as of now, we’re not very secure in our green energy capacity building.

Interviewer #3: [*sounds unconvinced*]

Interviewer #3 was clearly unconvinced, but I know I had a right point. Possibly it did not fit the discussion and sounded cacophonous. But what’s done is done.

Interviewer #2: Can you tell me about the China+1 policy?

Me: Ma’am, I can’t recall having heard this phrase.

Interviewer #2: You must have heard of it. You know about the US-China trade war?

Me: Oh, I get what you mean! It’s a policy whereby companies are trying to move some of their manufacturing out of China, because of risk of concentration as well as risks arising from the trade war.

Interviewer #2: Yes, exactly.

Me: Ma’am, many countries are trying to get some benefit out of this. Even India has been trying hard, and some companies have indeed opened manufacturing facilities – I’ve heard of a few around Agra. But due to superior Ease of Doing Business, countries like Vietnam have been much more successful, and have been able to draw most of the manufacturing space vacated by China. So we have a long way to go.

Interviewer #2: [*looks satisfied*]

At this point I don’t exactly remember what Interviewer #1 said, but it led to the following response.

Me: We are trying to import cotton from the US, as costs of certain raw cotton are higher in India. Since it’s cheaper to get that from the US, importing will help the industry overall.

Interviewer #1:  Oh, you’re referring to the theory of comparative advantage in trade.

Me: Yes, sir, exactly.

I feel this part of the interview went well for me overall, albeit a bit clunkily.

6. About mathematics and my background in general

Interviewer #3: So, Sameer, you have studied maths. Can you tell us about some idea or concept in maths that all of us can understand? [*waving his hand across the panel*]

I interpreted the request as: Explain something to us that we, as normal people, can comprehend, and not something abstruse from the arcane field of maths.

Me: Please allow me a moment to think.

I used this facility twice in the interview.

Me: Let me discuss sequences and convergence. We can have an infinite sequence of numbers [*drawing everything in the air using both hands*] … and though each number differs from the last, there is a different value that all are getting closer and closer to, and we say that the sequence is converging to that “limit”.

Interviewer #3: Let’s take this example: I start with a certain amount of money. I put it into such a fund that it doubles in a year. But instead of calculating and adding the interest at one go, it gets compounded over 6 months. Now I keep reducing the period in which it compounds. What do I end up with?

Me: Sir, we will get 2.718 [*takes a pause*] times the initial amount, of course.

Interviewer #3: And this number is called…?

Me: e.

Interviewer #3 (visibly pleased): Yes.

Interviewer: You have written that you took Statistics tutorials for 1st year students, what was that all about?

Me: So it was a 1st term course, and the course is quite hard for people who are not from a maths background. Therefore, in addition to normal classes, some students (who scored high in the course in the 1st year) get a chance to take tutorials for it when they reach 2nd year, for the new 1st year students.

Interviewer: How did you do in the tutorials? What were the topics?

Interviewer: It was a pretty small group of 5-10 PwD students. We used to assemble in an empty classroom and I would pick a few problems that I would discuss with them and slowly solve on the blackboard, making sure that everyone was following. The material was essentially the common probability distributions (such as binomial and Poisson) and basic estimation theory, including interval estimation.

Interviewer #2: Sameer, you’ve written that you worked on a term paper on the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code.

Me: [*nods*]

Interviewer #2: What can be improved in the Code, according to you?

Me: Ma’am, primarily by comparing the IBC to the codes used by other countries, and by studying some specific IBC cases, we found shortcomings regarding group companies and cross-border insolvency cases. [*talks a little about what these two really mean*]

Interviewer #2: But with the recent updates to IBC, these issues have been addressed.

Me: Yes, ma’am, I did see that in the news. These issues had persisted for many years.

End.

My views on how the interview transpired:

(a) There was a big focus on my want to join the RBI, as if they wanted to be sure that I truly desired this rather than just treating it as a thing to achieve and then move on. That's why this matter came up again and again, and ultimately I was NOT fully convincing. But this couldn't be helped — I got the same reaction in an interview with a certain PSU; perhaps my reasons don't entirely align with what was expected. I’m not a terribly ambitious person... Perhaps high ambition is expected from an IIM-C guy, but the fact remains that IIM-C is a great college even for mid-ambitious folks. I cherish my leisure and small things in life, and I had a higher chance of achieving  those things through IIM-C than through an ordinary MBA college.

(b) All the interviewers were overall quite nice, smiling, and humble. Since I got plenty of room to answer in my own way and desired direction, I have little to complain about. They did interrupt my answers a lot of times though – something that’s hard to recall and put into transcription, as the brain naturally smoothens out its memories.

C. RBI Grade B (DSIM) preparation, 2025

C.1. Phase I + Phase II

The recruitments for DSIM (Department of Statistics and Information Management) and DEPR are conducted concurrently with the Generalist recruitment by the RBI. The eligibility criterion for DSIM is an M.Sc. in Statistics or Mathematics, among others [please check the notification if interested]. The exam syllabus mostly follows B.Sc. (Statistics), with topics from AI/ML and Database Management added since Panel Year 2025.

My background is in Mathematics, not Statistics.

Realizing that the exam syllabus is actually just of the B.Sc. level and NOT the M.Sc. level (which one might suspect from the eligibility criteria), it was not overly difficult to cover for me. I should highlight the following aspects:

  1. The Statistics folks anyway spend a lot of time learning just Math – be it real analysis (≈ calculus), measure theory, or linear algebra. I was already comfortable with these topics.
  2. Measure theory, in particular, is important in college-level Math to build the theory of the (Lebesgue) integral. Since probability spaces are simply a special type of measure space, many probability-theoretic concepts were readily accessible to me.
  3. Probability theory is also central in quantum mechanics, on which I had several courses in my Integrated M.Sc., and in which I have always maintained a personal interest.
  4. I had studied Probability & Statistics as one course in my Integrated M.Sc., and further in my M.B.A.

This means the functional skills to handle and manipulate probability distributions and statistical tests were already there with me, albeit in a quirky mixture of quintessential rigor (coming from pure math) and superficial applications (coming from MBA). The mid-part – consisting of the development of statistical frameworks and tools – was missing, and will remain incomplete for years.

Anyhow, the method of preparation was twofold:

  1. Find out as many PYQs as possible and understand their solutions fully, so as to be able to tackle questions on those patterns. I extensively used Gemini for this, and it was accurate and helpful 90% of the time. My biggest challenge with Gemini was that it tended to miss the easy path through a problem, and to neglect telling me important things that were not explicitly asked for.
  2. Study some topics from the books. I couldn’t find one suitable book on Statistics that I enjoyed – but I used these somewhat: [DETAILS TO BE FILLED].

The PYQs used were of the ISS (Indian Statistical Service), CSIR-NET, and GATE.

D. The other examinations

I feel my 2024 preparation for RBI Grade B (Generalist) as well as NABARD Grade A and IRDAI Grade A somewhat helped me in 2025 as well. In what way? Mostly in terms of knowing what all to study and what all to skip.

I hardly prepared for SEBI Grade A (Generalist and Research streams) in 2025, but I did give plenty of attention to IFSCA Grade A, as I was attracted by the location (Ahnedabad). As hilariously easy as Phase I was, Phase II was demonic. I’m not a person with a strong memory power.

E. Other questions asked to me on Reddit etc.

Q 1. Did work experience help in the selection?

Honestly, I doubt that any marks would be assigned to work experience of the candidates. But in terms of confidence and ability to communicate in the interview, it helped. Hard to quantify.

If you have a mature conversational style out of college itself, there's nothing to be concerned about. If not, some practice with friends will be desirable.

Q 2. What’s the difference between the Generalist and DSIM work profiles? Which exam did you find to be easier?

Loosely speaking, in the Generalist stream they can put you into any department, but Supervision is the most common one AFAIK.

In DSIM, you always stay in DSIM (Department of Statistics and Information Management). Limited scope of work and requires specialized knowledge. Preference heavily dependent on interest.

I found the difficulties of both to be in the same ballpark, as I made roughly the same amount of silly mistakes in both, and fell short of some low-hanging fruit to roughly the same extent in both. Like I could easily have gotten 10 more marks in ESI, I could have gotten 10 more marks in DSIM Phase I [and remember, DSIM adds Phase I marks in calculating the final marks]. And I nearly made it in the Generalist stream as well, missing the final cutoff by merely 3 marks.

Q 2. Do gap years matter? Does X Y Z degree or certification matter?

No and no.

But be ready to defend your decisions and your aptitude on anything you let slip. The interviewers will drill down on anything that catches their eyes.

From your graduation (and post-graduation, if any) degrees, please have a schematic understanding of the field as a whole, and in addition an in-depth understanding of how it touches on matters of interest to the RBI – such as economics, statistics, and geopolitics – and possibly on day-to-day matters. In my case (see Section B.3), they asked me to tell them about some interesting idea in maths that they could understand: I had to take a moment but came up with something decent, but there were better answers possible; I could have brought up game theory (which underlies decision making in all sorts of scenarios) or optimization theory (which plays a role in economics, to find optimal values of, say, the repo rate). Once the discussion entered such a topic, I could have spoken vigorously on that.
